# Sample: quote follow-up for small repair businesses

**Fictional demonstration — not current market research.** Every organisation, observation, amount, and scenario below is invented. No interviews, purchases, deployments, or outreach occurred. This illustrates how to reason with uncertain evidence; it must not be cited as evidence about a real market.

## Decision and provisional recommendation

A fictional solo builder has two weeks and a $300 experiment budget. They want to decide whether to offer quote follow-up as a service or build software for independent repair shops.

Start with a bounded, manually delivered pilot. The most important uncertainty is whether shop owners will pay to improve follow-up. Automating reminders before observing that buying behaviour would leave the central question unanswered.

## Buyer and job

The assumed user is the person preparing repair quotes; the assumed payer is the shop owner. The trigger is an unanswered quote. Today, the imagined workflow is to revisit a spreadsheet and send messages manually. The job is to get a clear customer response without repeatedly checking each quote. These roles and the workflow must be confirmed in real interviews.

## What the evidence would and would not support

| Fictional input | Classification | What it could support if observed | What remains unproven |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| An imagined owner spends two hours weekly checking open quotes | Scenario assumption | A recurring workflow worth observing | Whether the time estimate is accurate or costly |
| Three imagined interviewees like a reminder demo | Scenario assumption | Interest in the presentation | Payment, retention, or deployment |
| An imagined supplier lists a $40 monthly reminder product | Fictional price anchor | A possible alternative to compare | Actual prices or transactions in a real market |
| No signed pilot or paid invoice exists in this demonstration | Demonstration limitation | No payment evidence is being claimed | Willingness to pay remains entirely untested |

A real investigation would find dated primary sources and inspect the buyer's actual process. It would also seek counter-evidence: perhaps existing tools already solve the problem, or unanswered quotes are mostly lost for reasons reminders cannot change.

## Commercial routes

| Route | First test | Distribution | Delivery burden | Evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Service-first | Deliver one tightly scoped pilot | Direct conversations with owners | Manual time per customer | Payment and a repeatable process |
| Small software product | Prototype one follow-up flow | Identify a reachable niche first | Product, integration, and support | Repeated demand and paid commitment |
| Existing-tool setup | Configure a tool the buyer already uses | Partner with a local provider | Setup and training | Buyer preference for implementation help |

Given the fictional time constraint, service-first creates the fastest opportunity to observe the work. Existing-tool setup could be preferable if interviews reveal that owners mainly need help configuring tools they already pay for.

## Pricing and economics: hypotheses only

A two-week pilot at **$150 is a pricing hypothesis**, not a market price. If delivery takes three hours at an assumed internal cost of $30/hour, plus $10 of tooling, the illustrative contribution is `$150 − $90 − $10 = $50`, before acquisition, tax, and overhead. At five delivery hours it becomes `−$10`. Both calculations use invented inputs.

If a shop saves one hour weekly, that is capacity released. It is not necessarily a cash saving, and it does not establish extra repair revenue. Measure the owner's actual use of that time before making an ROI claim.

## Minimum learning experiment

After the builder authorizes outreach, aim for five owner conversations in two weeks. Five is a planning target, not a conversion forecast. Ask about the last unanswered quote, the current follow-up process, and previous spending on the problem; avoid asking only whether the idea sounds useful.

Offer at most one pilot covering a bounded set of quotes, with the owner approving messages and any necessary data access. Request the hypothetical $150 payment before delivery. Use fictional or redacted data for the demonstration.

Proceed to another pilot only if an owner pays, the work stays within the three-hour delivery assumption, and the owner can identify a measured improvement worth continuing to buy. If nobody commits after five qualified conversations, revisit the offer and buyer segment rather than building software. Stop if access cannot be obtained appropriately or delivery economics remain negative.
